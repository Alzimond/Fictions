For eleven nights, the municipal sleep archive received nothing but silence. The machines were working. Their needles moved. Each morning, a technician removed twelve hours of apparently empty tape, sealed it in a grey sleeve, and filed it under the name of a district that had, according to the equipment, forgotten how to dream. Nobody suggested stopping the recorders. A gap in the record would have been harder to explain than a record of nothing. The distinction mattered to the people whose signatures appeared on the sleeves, even before it mattered to anyone else.

I arrived on the twelfth morning with a visitor's pass and a notebook the guard asked me to open. He inspected its blank pages, handed it back, and pointed toward a staircase. The lift was reserved for materials whose condition might be affected by movement. On the landing, a cleaner was polishing a brass plaque. I asked whether she had noticed anything unusual. She said the building had become easier to clean. Nobody stayed late to listen anymore, and people who went home on time left fewer cups behind.

## 01 / WHAT THE CITY USED TO KEEP

The archive began as a medical service. Patients waking in distress could compare their recollections with a recording and identify the moment when a familiar room became an unfamiliar one. At least, that was the explanation preserved in the opening brochure. Later brochures described other benefits: research, education, cultural memory, the preservation of threatened languages. Each generation found a reason to maintain the machinery. None found a reason to revisit the original agreement that permitted the city to collect what its inhabitants experienced while unable to refuse.

Collection was automatic, although participation was officially voluntary. A household could disconnect its receiver by visiting an office open on alternate afternoons, presenting two forms of identification, and demonstrating that every registered resident agreed. People who lived alone were asked for a witness. People without a registered address were collected anyway, because the system could detect a sleeping mind more readily than it could determine an administrative boundary. This discrepancy had been acknowledged in six reviews. The recommendations from the seventh were due that winter.

The recordings were not pictures in the usual sense. They were arrangements of pressure, colour, interrupted language, and relationships between objects. A trained listener could follow a room as it became a coastline without deciding where the transformation happened. Untrained listeners often reported nausea. The archive discouraged them from listening alone, then charged for supervised appointments. Its educational department published small selections in which transitions had been softened and names removed. These were the dreams that entered schoolbooks: comprehensible, attributed, and already arranged to support an account of the past.

Very little of the collection had ever been heard. Storage grew faster than the staff, and the staff grew faster than the budget for interpretation. Most recordings travelled from receiver to shelf without encountering a conscious listener. Nevertheless, the existence of those shelves changed how people spoke. A forgotten detail was recoverable. An argument about a recollection could, in principle, be settled. Somewhere beneath every uncertain conversation stood the reassuring possibility of a more complete version, stored by someone who had no personal interest in being believed.

That reassurance had always been unevenly distributed. Appointments required time, translations required money, and some districts produced clearer recordings than others. People whose dreams arrived damaged learned to describe themselves cautiously. They were not necessarily less certain about what they remembered. They simply knew that certainty without an accompanying record sounded different across a service counter. The archive did not invent this distinction. It gave the distinction a room, a procedure, and a number that could be quoted when an application was refused.

## 02 / AN INSTRUMENT FOR ABSENCE

Mara Venn supervised the northern receiving room. She had worked there for nineteen years and could identify a failing motor before its warning light came on. When I found her, she was listening to a recorder through a narrow metal probe pressed against its casing. The sound she wanted was mechanical. Electronic diagnostics reported that everything was normal, but normal was a broad category, and she preferred to know which part of it she was dealing with. She offered me the probe. I heard a patient, almost domestic hum.

The first silent reel had not seemed important. A damaged transmitter, an empty apartment, a misplaced registration: there were many ordinary explanations. Then an entire street went quiet. By the third night the silence covered every northern district, though the boundaries of that region did not match the boundaries of any receiving circuit. Venn drew both maps on transparent sheets. Superimposed, they produced a complicated pattern that explained nothing. She kept them together anyway. Separating them made it too easy to mistake one kind of boundary for another.

Her team tested the equipment in stages. They replaced cables, exchanged receivers with another station, and played historical recordings through the incoming lines. Every test succeeded. A borrowed instrument recorded the same silence as the municipal one. A municipal instrument recorded an engineer's waking conversation without difficulty. The technicians could demonstrate what the system was capable of receiving. They could not demonstrate that the thing they expected to receive was still being sent. Their report made this distinction in its first paragraph. The published summary omitted it.

One worker suggested testing the empty tapes at a different speed. Another suggested reversing them. They tried both, along with methods that the manual explicitly advised against. Slower silence acquired a lower mechanical hiss. Reversed silence began where it had ended. These experiments were not foolish, Venn said. When the accepted method produces an impossible result, people need permission to examine the method before being asked to revise their understanding of the world. The difficulty was deciding when examination had become a way of postponing that revision.

On a desk beside the machines lay a page divided into two columns. The first listed things the team knew. The second listed things they had assumed. Over several days, entries had moved from left to right. The equipment was operational remained on the left. People were no longer dreaming had never been there. Venn noticed me reading it and turned the page over. It was an internal aid, she said, not an official conclusion. She had already been asked why a qualified technician needed to write down the difference.

## 03 / A STAIRCASE WITHOUT A TAPE

The first public contradiction came from a woman named Sera Dalm, who remembered carrying a table upstairs in a house she had never visited. She arrived at the archive expecting to purchase a copy. Her father had died that spring, and in the dream he had been holding the other end. The clerk could offer only a certificate confirming that the receiver assigned to her address had operated normally. Dalm asked whether the certificate meant the dream had not happened. The clerk said that was not a question the certificate was designed to answer.

Dalm returned the following afternoon with a drawing. The table was narrow, the stairwell curved, and a window appeared on a landing where she knew there should have been no exterior wall. She had marked her father's hand with an arrow. Not because she expected the clerk to recognise it, she explained, but because she wanted to establish which part mattered. The drawing was accepted as supplementary material. A receipt described it as a claimant's reconstruction. Dalm disliked the word claimant but kept the receipt inside the folded page.

Other visitors followed. A driver remembered a river travelling uphill. A child described a classroom in which the desks were occupied by different versions of her mother. A retired surveyor had dreamed an ordinary breakfast and wanted that fact recorded precisely because nothing unusual had occurred. Staff gave them the same form. The form provided three lines for the account and a box marked supporting evidence. When someone asked whether the account itself could count as evidence, the supervisor arranged a meeting to discuss inconsistent use of terminology.

Outside, the visitors began comparing stories. This was not a planned gathering. People simply remained after their appointments because somebody else had asked a question that sounded like their own. Dalm showed her staircase. A stranger recognised the window, or thought he did. They spent an hour arguing gently about the direction of the light. Neither had a recording. Neither could close the conversation by appealing to a more authoritative version. At the end, they agreed that the window could be familiar without the room being shared.

By evening, somebody had brought chairs. By the next afternoon, a vendor had moved a cart closer to the entrance. The archive objected to the obstruction, although the pavement had always accommodated queues. What had changed was that these people were no longer waiting for the institution to do something. They were using its doorstep for a purpose the institution had not authorised and could not easily describe as unrelated to its work. The guard moved the chairs twice, then began arranging them himself before the office opened.

## 04 / THE COST OF A COMPLETE ACCOUNT

The city issued its first statement on the sixth day. It described a temporary interruption in the delivery of nonessential services and assured residents that their data remained secure. Both claims required definitions that the statement did not supply. If dreams were nonessential, why had collection continued through three electricity shortages? If the data were secure, what exactly was being secured? The communications office answered that the physical holdings were unaffected. This was true, and also different from what most readers had understood the sentence to mean.

An emergency committee assembled representatives from health, infrastructure, culture, and records management. Each brought a different account of the archive's purpose. The health representative wanted reassurance for patients. Infrastructure wanted a measurable fault. Culture wanted protection for a public asset. Records management wanted to know whether a silent reel should receive the same retention period as a populated one. These were not absurd priorities. They were the priorities for which each person could be held responsible. Their incompatibility became visible only because the usual outcome had stopped arriving.

At the meeting I attended, a financial officer calculated the storage cost of continuing to preserve silence. She was interrupted by a curator who said absence could itself be historically significant. The officer agreed, then asked how many identical absences were needed to establish that significance. Nobody answered. Afterward she told me that she liked the archive and had used it herself. Her question was not whether memory deserved resources. It was which other public services should surrender resources to preserve something that nobody could yet explain how to distinguish.

A proposal emerged to retain one representative reel per district and certify the destruction of the rest. Venn opposed it. The team did not know that the reels were identical, she argued; they knew only that available instruments detected no meaningful difference. Her objection delayed the decision without resolving the budget. That evening, staff moved boxes into a corridor formerly reserved for exhibitions. A display about the city's first recorded dream disappeared behind a stack of the city's latest apparently empty ones. Visitors photographed the arrangement before anybody could remove it.

The photograph became popular because it seemed to offer a clear interpretation. The past was being buried by the present; the archive was drowning in silence; the city was hiding its origins. None of these descriptions explained why the boxes were there. They were there because the corridor had available floor space and a door wide enough for the trolley. Venn found the image useful nonetheless. People who had never thought about the archive were suddenly asking who worked inside it. Sometimes an inaccurate metaphor opened a conversation that an accurate inventory could not.

## 05 / THE UNSUPERVISED VERSION

The gathering outside acquired a notebook. It belonged to the vendor, who had previously used it to track supplies. On the first page, between orders for cups and milk, someone wrote a description of a station with no exits. More descriptions followed. The entries had no assigned numbers. Names were optional. Anyone could add a question beneath an account, but the writer of the account could decide whether to answer. This arrangement was revised several times without being given a formal title. Participants called the notebook the book because there was only one.

It was not an innocent solution. Some people wrote more confidently than others. Some descriptions attracted attention because they were beautiful, disturbing, or easy to repeat. A quiet account of a familiar kitchen nearly disappeared beneath a debate about a sea made of glass. Dalm began leaving blank pages between entries so that the amount of response would not determine the amount of space an account received. This did not create equality. It made one form of inequality visible enough for people to negotiate without first pretending it had been solved.

A student offered to make a searchable copy. Several people welcomed the idea. Others asked where the copy would live, who could change it, and whether deleting an entry from the notebook would remove it from the copy. The student had expected gratitude and instead found himself answering questions about custody. He stayed. Over the following days he helped develop a system in which each contributor could choose whether a page was copied. The resulting index contained deliberate gaps. Searching it could not reveal whether a missing account had never existed or had simply declined to travel.

The archive sent an observer. She introduced herself by name and asked permission to sit, which altered the discussion more effectively than her badge. She was interested in the practice of comparing uncertain recollections. The group was interested in whether she could obtain more chairs. Eventually both interests were addressed. Her report described a community-led interpretive resource. The vendor thought this sounded like something that would soon need an application. He asked her to keep the notebook's original name in the report. She did, in a footnote.

There were disagreements that the book could not hold. Two participants accused each other of borrowing details. Someone recognised a private experience in another person's entry and asked for it to be removed. A story circulated beyond the pavement after its contributor had requested that it remain there. These incidents did not prove the experiment had failed. They demonstrated that the old system's difficult questions had survived the failure of its machinery. A different arrangement could redistribute responsibility. It could not make responsibility disappear merely by calling itself informal.

## 06 / WHAT REMAINS PRIVATE

One evening I met a former interpreter who refused to give her name. She had spent a decade turning recordings into descriptions for people unable to tolerate direct playback. The work required judgement: where a scene began, whether a voice belonged to someone, how much ambiguity a client could bear. Officially, her descriptions were aids rather than replacements. In practice, they became the versions people remembered. She had sometimes wondered whether an archive that promised to preserve experience was manufacturing the form in which experience could later be recalled.

She did not welcome the silence. Patients had lost something useful, and workers might lose their jobs. But she resisted the assumption that restoring collection would restore an uncomplicated good. People often forgot their recordings could be heard by someone else. Consent forms did not change the strangeness of that encounter. She remembered a client who asked her to describe a room without mentioning the person inside it. The recording could not separate them. Her description could. She still did not know whether that had been an omission or a service.

When I asked whether dreams might belong to more than one person, she laughed, then apologised. Everyone wanted to begin with ownership, she said, because ownership promised a rule for deciding who could act. But experience was full of borrowed rooms, recognisable voices, gestures learned from people who had never agreed to become material. A dream could be intimate without being self-contained. The archive had treated each recording as an individual object because individual objects were easier to catalogue. That convenience had gradually acquired the appearance of a fact about minds.

Her own dreams had never been collected. She had disconnected her receiver before taking the job and renewed the exemption whenever it expired. Coworkers regarded this as eccentric rather than disloyal. Some had done the same. Inside the institution, the limitations of collection were ordinary knowledge. Outside, the existence of technical expertise was often taken as proof that the limitations had been overcome. She thought the silence might briefly bring those two understandings closer together. Then she asked me not to turn that hope into a prediction.

I returned to the pavement the following day and found Dalm closing the notebook. She no longer wanted to compare the staircase with anyone else's. Each retelling made it clearer, and that clarity had begun to trouble her. She could remember the drawing more readily than the dream. The window now had a frame she was sure she had added while explaining it. She intended to keep the page but stop improving it. Her father had carried one end of a table. For the moment, she wanted that unfinished recollection to be sufficient.

## 07 / THE SHAPE OF A RETURN

Nobody had agreed on what would count as recovery. The engineers wanted ordinary signals from every district. The health office wanted patients to resume their appointments. The committee wanted a date it could put in a report. Those outcomes might arrive together, but they might not. A single clear recording could demonstrate technical recovery while leaving most residents without access. Restored appointments could conceal damaged material. A report could announce the end of an interruption long before the people who had lived through it felt able to trust the service again.

I asked several residents whether they would reconnect a receiver if collection became optional in practice. Their answers depended on what they feared losing. One wanted her children to have records of a language they rarely heard while awake. Another wanted proof that a recurring experience had changed. A third liked knowing that something of his life existed beyond the rooms he could afford. None described the archive as perfect. Their attachment was to particular uses, and those uses were more specific than either the institution's promises or its critics' objections.

Others had quietly enjoyed the interruption. A man who worked in dispute resolution said that breakfast conversations had become less forensic. His partner no longer proposed booking an appointment whenever their recollections differed. They had not become more agreeable. They had become less certain that disagreement required a final version. He did not want this printed as advice. Some disagreements concerned matters for which evidence was essential, he said. He was speaking about their own breakfasts, not offering a principle for organising everyone else's life.

These distinctions complicated the campaign that had formed around the archive. A petition called for immediate restoration without alteration. Another called for collection to cease permanently. Between them stood people who wanted restoration with different terms, or continued investigation without automatic collection, or access to the old material without permission for new material to be taken. Their positions were difficult to fit on banners. They appeared instead in long conversations near the cart, where the vendor sometimes had to remind speakers that an audience could be interested and still need to go to work.

An electrician proposed a physical switch beside every receiver. It would be obvious, accessible, and free to operate. The technical department noted that switches could fail. A resident replied that offices could fail too. The exchange was repeated widely because it sounded conclusive, although it settled little about installation, shared households, or the status of recordings made before the switch was fitted. What it did establish was a different starting point. Refusal might be something the system supported as an ordinary action, rather than an exception a resident had to justify to a person behind a desk.

Venn thought the switch was feasible. She also thought it would expose disagreements that the existing procedure had hidden. In a shared room, whose choice would determine whether collection occurred? Could a parent decide for a child? Would a landlord be allowed to install a receiver among essential utilities? These were questions the city had already answered indirectly, through defaults and administrative inconvenience. Making the choice visible would not create the conflict. It would make the conflict harder to mistake for the natural operation of a machine. She hoped the committee would understand that difference before asking her team to estimate the cost. For her, a functioning service would have to include a way of declining it without disappearing from the city's concern. Otherwise the proposed recovery would restore the equipment while leaving the oldest fault exactly where it had always been, outside the scope of any maintenance report.

## 08 / A PROVISIONAL MORNING

On the seventeenth night, one northern receiver produced a signal. It lasted less than a second and contained a pattern that resembled the beginning of a voice. The team did not announce a recovery. They checked the line, the clock, and the receiver's maintenance history. By morning, they had identified a brief crossover from an adjacent circuit. The incident was ordinary and irrelevant to the missing dreams. It nevertheless changed the mood in the room. People who had learned to work around the absence discovered how quickly they could become tired of accommodating it.

Venn had begun sleeping badly. She disliked the irony and asked me not to make too much of it. Her concern was practical: a tired technician made mistakes, and a mistake could produce a false result that would consume days of investigation. She shortened her shifts and appointed a colleague to review her notes. In a building dedicated to experiences beyond conscious control, this small admission of limitation seemed unusually deliberate. She was not abandoning the search. She was refusing to let the importance of the search excuse unreliable work.

The committee authorised six more weeks of collection and delayed the destruction proposal. The announcement was described as a precautionary measure. It satisfied nobody completely. Staff wanted a longer commitment; other services wanted a cost ceiling; people outside wanted their own activities recognised without being absorbed. The decision was less dramatic than the photograph of the boxes, but more consequential. For six weeks, no one would have to pretend that uncertainty had ended simply because an administrative deadline had arrived. After that, the question would have to be asked again.

Before I left, Venn showed me a recorder she had taken out of the network. It stood on a shelf, disconnected from every incoming line. She ran it for an hour each night and stored the result separately. This was a control, she explained, not an experiment in catching unregistered dreams. She wanted a reliable account of the sound the machine made by itself. The distinction was important to her. It was also the kind of distinction that would be easy to lose once the image of a solitary recorder entered a story.

Outside, the vendor was closing his cart. The notebook had been put away, and the chairs were stacked beside the guard's desk. Nothing in the square looked like the beginning of a new institution. Tomorrow, people would return with questions, impatience, and descriptions that changed as they were spoken. Beneath them, the machines would continue to turn. Somewhere, the city might still be producing everything it had learned to expect. Here, for now, there was no complete version waiting to settle the matter. There were only people deciding what to keep, and whom to believe, until morning.
