At 06:14, a square in the southern district filled with light. The official schedule said it should remain in shadow until winter. Its shops were shuttered, its fountain had been drained, and the only person present was a cleaner who had arrived to collect yesterday's dust. She stopped beside the fountain with one hand on her trolley. The warmth lasted seven minutes. Then a precise edge moved across the paving, climbed the opposite wall, and vanished. Nothing remained that she could put in a report except the time.

The cleaner's name was Anja Pell. The next morning she brought a chair. On the third morning there were six chairs, and by the end of the week someone was selling coffee beside the old ticket kiosk. Nobody had advertised a gathering. Pell mentioned the light to a colleague, the colleague told a neighbour, and a delivery rider paused long enough to ask what everyone was waiting for. Their presence travelled along routes that the district's redevelopment maps did not show. The square had been described as empty for years. It had never been unreachable.

## 01 / HOW MORNING ARRIVES

For sixty years, the city had purchased part of its daylight from an orbital mirror. Natural sunlight reached the upper terraces without assistance, but the lower districts lay behind a ridge of industrial buildings, retaining walls, and the mountain itself. Early plans proposed demolishing the structures that blocked the sky. Later plans calculated that redirecting light would cost less. The mirror became a compromise between geography and property. It allowed the city to describe the problem as solved without asking which buildings should move or who had profited from their remaining in place.

The mirror was not a single surface. It was an assembly of adjustable panels, each large enough to reflect a different portion of the solar disc toward a different receiving zone. From the ground it usually appeared as an unremarkable point of light. Its scale became apparent only in technical drawings, where service vehicles resembled punctuation marks along the edges. Children learned its orbital period before they learned the names of nearby mountains. The mountains were obstacles. The mirror was infrastructure, and infrastructure appeared on examinations because it required people who knew how to maintain it.

Contracts divided the day into blocks. Hospitals received priority, followed by schools, public gardens, and residential corridors. Commercial districts could purchase additional intervals. Industrial premises sometimes sold unused allocations back to the utility. The resulting schedule reflected need, bargaining power, historical agreements, and the difficulty of changing software written when some of those categories meant different things. It was possible to explain every individual allocation. It was much harder to explain the arrangement as a whole without treating its history as a sufficient reason for its continuation.

Residents rarely saw the contracts. They knew that a bedroom brightened at a certain hour, that plants survived on one balcony and not another, that a stairwell became unexpectedly beautiful during a brief overlap between two receiving zones. These experiences were no less real because their causes were administrative. People developed habits around them. A grandmother timed her letters to the light crossing her table. A mechanic left polished parts on a sill to inspect their surfaces. The timetable became a form of weather, except that someone could be invoiced for changing it.

The square occupied a gap between zones. It had once served workers from a printing plant, then passengers from a station that closed when the plant moved. Its allocation expired after the final lease ended. The fountain was disconnected to save water, although it had recirculated most of what it used. The shops remained in private ownership while the paving remained a municipal responsibility. Each arrangement was documented. Taken together, they made it surprisingly difficult to answer the simple question of who should have noticed that the square was still there.

## 02 / SEVEN MINUTES WITHOUT AN ACCOUNT

Pell kept a notebook because her supervisor required precise records of cleaning supplies. Between entries for detergent and replacement brushes, she wrote the beginning and end of the light. For nine mornings, the interval varied by less than a second. Clouds changed its colour but did not prevent its arrival. Rain made the paving shine. On one morning she held a piece of cardboard above the notebook and traced the moving boundary. Later, she discovered that the mark was less useful than the memory of how slowly she had needed to move her hand.

Her supervisor initially asked her to stop gathering people during working hours. Pell pointed out that she finished the square before the light arrived and took her break afterward. The schedule supported her account. He then worried that the chairs created an obstruction. She arranged them along the drained fountain, leaving more space than the parked maintenance vehicles usually allowed. He did not return to the subject. This was not an endorsement. It was the small, practical retreat that becomes possible when a rule has been satisfied more carefully than its enforcer expected.

The utility first learned about the light from a photograph. Someone had included the mirror's customer service address in a message asking why an abandoned square received sunlight while an occupied courtyard nearby did not. A technician checked the allocation database. There was no active customer at that location, no scheduled trial, and no emergency override. The projected beam could be identified in the orbital logs, but the corresponding instruction did not appear in the billing system. Light had arrived successfully. The institution lacked a category for that particular kind of success.

A preliminary statement called it a calibration error. This seemed plausible. Small adjustments at orbital distance could move a beam across several streets. But the engineers found no corresponding shortage elsewhere, and the panels involved had not drifted from their recorded positions. The error was therefore somewhere between the command, its interpretation, and the account of what the command was supposed to accomplish. The public statement remained unchanged while the internal description grew longer. Residents were told that investigation was ongoing and advised not to rely on the continuation of the service.

Pell read that advice aloud beside the fountain. Nobody thought continuation was guaranteed. They had seen a station close, a fountain empty, and a square lose its name from transit maps. The suggestion that official provision was dependable while unexplained provision was precarious did not fit their experience. They kept coming, but several began bringing portable lamps for the minutes before sunrise. The lamps were unnecessary once the beam arrived. Their presence nevertheless made the gathering feel less like a group waiting to be granted permission to see one another.

## 03 / THE MAN WHO USED TO TURN THE SKY

Ilan Sato retired from the control service twelve years earlier. His former colleagues still called him when a new interface concealed a function they remembered being able to use. He answered reluctantly, not because he disliked helping but because the calls encouraged management to treat his memory as a substitute for documentation. When I visited his apartment, he was repairing a radio that could no longer receive any licensed station. He said the repair was straightforward. Deciding what to listen to afterward was a separate problem.

Sato had heard about the square from his daughter. He visited before agreeing to discuss it and spent the first morning watching the shadow of a signpost. The beam's edges were unusually clean. A drifting panel would have produced a less stable boundary, he thought, although he cautioned that the modern assembly differed from the one he had maintained. Retirement had not frozen the machinery. It had only frozen the portion of its history for which he could claim direct responsibility. He wanted that limitation included whenever his opinion was quoted.

His first suggestion concerned old commissioning routines. Before a receiving zone opened, operators used an unoccupied reference area to test alignment. Some routines survived long after their original purpose disappeared because removing them required proving that nothing else depended on them. A software revision could expose an instruction that had remained dormant for decades. That would make the square's illumination neither a random malfunction nor a deliberate gift. It would be an old practical arrangement finding a route through a system whose present operators no longer recognised its name.

The theory was attractive because it offered a solvable problem. Find the routine, identify its dependencies, and decide whether to remove it. Sato distrusted its attractiveness. A good explanation could become an excuse to stop looking. He asked for historical commissioning maps rather than recent summaries and found that the square had once been designated Reference Surface C. The designation appeared in a manual beside instructions for checking atmospheric scatter. He could not establish whether that routine was still running. The correspondence was a lead, not the conclusion people wanted it to be.

He brought a copy of the map to the fountain. Pell looked at the label and laughed. She had cleaned Reference Surface C for fourteen years without knowing it had a technical name. Sato explained what the surface had been used for. She explained which drains overflowed and where the paving became slippery after rain. Neither description replaced the other. Together, they made the square more specific than it had been in either the utility's database or the redevelopment proposal that described it as a parcel awaiting an appropriate future use.

## 04 / PEOPLE WHO CAN AFFORD TO WAIT

The gathering remained small, but small was not the same as representative. Its regular visitors tended to work nearby, start late, or have finished a night shift. Parents accompanying children to school could stay only briefly. A wheelchair user found that the shortest route included a locked service gate and stopped coming after two attempts. The light itself was free, but arriving beneath it required time, access, and a body able to manage the available route. These costs were easy to overlook in photographs of people sitting together without tickets.

Pell began asking newcomers how they had reached the square. She was not collecting data in an official sense. She wanted to know why some people returned and others did not. The answers changed practical arrangements. A shopkeeper agreed to open a passage through his yard. Someone marked the least uneven route with small chalk arrows. The service gate remained locked because the department responsible for its key required a request from the department responsible for its hinges. A resident wrote both departments the same letter and delivered it in person.

The coffee seller did well enough to attract a second vendor. For a week, their competition made the square livelier. Then they disagreed about where carts could stand and whether customers should be allowed to occupy chairs without buying anything. No one had established rules because the absence of rules had initially felt like part of the gift. Pell refused to let the chairs become part of either business. She could enforce that refusal only because she owned several chairs and knew the people who owned the others. Informality had acquired a structure of its own.

A photographer offered to document the gathering for a magazine. Some welcomed the attention; others disliked the idea of becoming an illustration of urban resilience. The phrase appeared in the photographer's description before anyone on the square used it. A night porter asked whether the magazine would mention that he had spent the previous hour waiting for a delayed bus. His presence was not a lifestyle choice. He liked the sunlight, but he would have preferred to be home. The photographer stayed long enough to include the bus stop in the frame.

These complications did not make the gathering less valuable. They made its value harder to describe in a single uplifting sentence. People shared food, argued about space, helped strangers, and occasionally behaved selfishly. The beam did not improve anyone's character. It provided a reason to encounter people whose schedules usually kept them apart. Whatever followed depended on what they did with those encounters. The utility could supply seven minutes of illumination. It could neither guarantee the relationships that formed beneath it nor take reasonable credit for all of them.

## 05 / THE PRICE OF SOMETHING ALREADY HERE

The first formal proposal was to create a temporary customer account for the square. An officer presented this as a helpful solution: the light could continue while engineers investigated its source, and residents would have a recognised point of contact. The proposal also required a named representative, a payment method, and acceptance of conditions permitting interruption without compensation. Several visitors asked why a technical investigation required them to become customers. The officer said it did not. Continued service did. The distinction was procedurally clear and socially difficult to explain.

There was no obvious person to sign. Pell had discovered the light but did not represent everyone who used it. The vendors were present daily but had commercial interests. A neighbourhood association offered to act, although its membership covered streets beyond the square and excluded some of the people most regularly there. Choosing a representative would decide more than who received a bill. It would establish who could speak when schedules changed, who received warnings, and whose account of the gathering became the one the city could recognise.

The proposed tariff was modest when divided among a large group and expensive when divided among the few willing to commit. That arithmetic concealed the larger question of why an unused beam had a price. The utility explained that every allocation carried maintenance and coordination costs, even when it displaced no other customer. Critics replied that those costs already existed. Both statements could be true. Neither determined how the costs should be distributed. A technical account of expenditure could inform that decision without making the decision disappear into a spreadsheet.

Sato attended the public meeting and spoke against treating the mirror as costless. Its panels needed repair. Its operators deserved wages. Launching replacement components required resources that could have been used elsewhere. He also opposed using those facts to imply that every interval needed an individual buyer. The city funded other shared services without assigning a customer to each moment of use. The disagreement was about the terms of a common provision, he said, and should be conducted in those terms rather than hidden behind an argument over whether sunlight was naturally free.

Afterward, several people accused him of taking both sides. He seemed untroubled. He had maintained equipment long enough to know that identifying two real constraints did not amount to indecision. The difficult work began when neither could be wished away. The square required care, and its visitors should not have to invent a private organisation simply to prevent something useful from being switched off. He wanted the city to acknowledge both propositions before choosing a solution. Otherwise, the chosen solution would spend years rediscovering whichever proposition had been excluded.

## 06 / A MAP THAT CHANGES ITS READER

The investigation eventually found an inherited instruction in the alignment software. Its identifier matched the old reference surface, but the reason for its recent activation remained uncertain. A change in orbital timing had exposed a narrow interval previously occupied by another task. The controller used that interval to perform an old check. This account explained the timing and the absence of a displaced allocation. It did not explain why the routine had survived successive reviews, though the engineers suspected the answer was less mysterious than the public discussion had made it sound.

One reviewer had marked the instruction for removal years earlier. Another had noted that its dependencies were undocumented. A later migration preserved all instructions not explicitly approved for deletion. Each decision was defensible within its immediate circumstances. Their accumulation produced seven minutes of light in a square whose public purpose had been allowed to lapse. There was no hidden benefactor and no rebellious machine. There was a history of people working under deadlines, preserving unfamiliar functions because breaking an unknown dependency seemed more dangerous than keeping an obsolete instruction.

The discovery changed the newspaper coverage. Stories about an inexplicable gift became stories about bureaucratic neglect. Pell disliked both versions for the same reason: they made the square interesting only through something happening elsewhere. People had been crossing it, cleaning it, and maintaining its remaining connections throughout the years when it received no attention. The old routine mattered because it brought light. It had not brought the place into existence. She asked a reporter to begin the next article with the cleaner's route rather than the orbit. The reporter agreed, then described the route inaccurately.

The engineers could now disable the instruction with confidence. They recommended doing so, while noting that an equivalent scheduled allocation could be created through the normal process. The distinction made sense from a maintenance perspective. Hidden dependencies were undesirable even when their effects were welcome. Residents worried that removal would be immediate while replacement would become another application awaiting review. Their concern was supported by several unrelated experiences. The city proposed a transition period. For the first time, an official document described uninterrupted access as something worth preserving during a technical correction.

At the square, Sato unfolded a new map beside the old one. The physical outlines were nearly identical. The labels were not. Reference Surface C had become a proposed shared receiving area. The change looked small, but it altered what questions a reader would ask. A reference surface needed accurate measurements. A shared area needed access, maintenance, and some account of whose needs its schedule served. The beam had not become warmer. The description around it had become capable of acknowledging the people already standing in its path.

## 07 / THE SHADOW NEXT DOOR

Not everyone welcomed the proposal. Residents of an adjacent courtyard had applied for additional light three times and received refusals based on cost. They asked why an accidental gathering deserved a service they had requested through the prescribed channels. Some visitors to the square initially treated the objection as hostility. It was easier to defend a fragile success against an apparent opponent than to recognise a competing claim with an equally plausible history. Pell visited the courtyard one afternoon. Its windows faced a wall so close that weather was something residents inferred from wet footsteps in the entrance.

The courtyard could not receive the square's beam without significant changes to its angle. Sharing the seven minutes was therefore not a simple matter of dividing the interval. The utility presented that fact as a constraint, which it was. Residents heard it as another refusal, which their experience made understandable. An engineer brought a scale model to a meeting and demonstrated several alternatives. None was free. Two were feasible. The demonstration did not resolve the allocation dispute, but it replaced an apparently absolute impossibility with choices whose costs could be debated.

The visitors agreed to support the courtyard's application. Agreement came slowly and did not erase suspicion. Some feared that expanding the discussion would delay the square's arrangement indefinitely. Others thought a success that depended on ignoring the next street would be difficult to defend. They settled on a letter that stated both claims separately and opposed using either as a reason to reject the other. The letter did not promise solidarity as a permanent condition. It recorded a specific commitment that could later be checked against what the signatories actually did.

During this period, clouds reduced the beam to a pale rectangle for several mornings. Attendance declined, then stabilised. People still talked beside the fountain, though fewer photographs appeared online. A volunteer repaired a loose chair. The service gate was finally opened after the two departments discovered that neither had a record requiring it to remain locked. These developments were less spectacular than direct sunlight. They also remained useful after the seven minutes ended. The gathering had begun to produce changes that did not depend on the mirror continuing to behave in precisely the same way.

Pell resisted being described as its leader. She had preferences, influence, and a reliable knowledge of where spare chairs were stored. Those qualities did not entitle her to settle every disagreement. When a visitor asked her to decide where a new noticeboard should stand, she suggested asking the people who used the passage it might obstruct. This was slower than giving an answer. Sometimes it produced an answer she disliked. She considered that evidence that the square was becoming a shared place rather than an extension of her original discovery.

## 08 / A MORNING WITH CONDITIONS

The city approved a six-month public allocation while a wider review examined access in the lower districts. The interval remained seven minutes, though its start would shift slightly with orbital conditions. Maintenance funding came from an existing public-space budget. There was no new customer account. The decision was provisional, vulnerable to later priorities, and narrower than several campaigners had wanted. It nevertheless meant the next morning's light would arrive because somebody had accepted responsibility for providing it, rather than because an obsolete instruction had escaped deletion.

The transition occurred on a Thursday. Sato came early and stood beside Pell without explaining what he expected to see. The beam arrived several seconds later than usual, moved across the paving, and warmed the chairs. Nothing visibly marked the difference between an inherited command and a deliberate allocation. There was no announcement from the sky. An engineer sent a short message confirming that the new schedule had executed correctly. Pell read it, put her phone away, and asked whether anyone had seen the cloth she used to dry the fountain's edge.

A journalist asked Sato whether the city had learned a lesson. He said cities did not learn in the same way people did. They changed procedures, retained records, replaced staff, and sometimes repeated decisions whose consequences had been carefully documented. If the episode mattered, its importance would need to survive those ordinary transitions. He wanted the commissioning maps preserved alongside the public allocation agreement and the letters from the courtyard. The next person reviewing the schedule should encounter the reasons for it, not merely a mysterious instruction that seemed safe to remove.

On my final visit, the coffee cart was late. A child counted the chairs and found one missing. Pell had lent it to a neighbour whose table was being repaired. The beam reached the fountain while she explained this, and for a moment the conversation paused. Everyone turned slightly toward the warmth. Then the child asked when the chair would come back. The question restored the scale of the place: an orbital machine, a municipal promise, a borrowed object, and people who would still need to make arrangements after the light moved on.

Seven minutes later, the boundary crossed the paving and climbed the wall. Some visitors left immediately. Others remained to finish conversations that no longer required illumination to justify them. The courtyard's application was still pending. The six-month review had not yet begun. There was no final agreement about what a city owed its mornings. There was, however, a square that could no longer be described as empty without somebody objecting from experience. Its chairs stood in shadow, waiting for the next arrival. Somewhere, this had already happened. Here, it would have to be made possible again.
